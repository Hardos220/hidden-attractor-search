\section{Method}\label{sec:method}

The method is a portfolio of three arms: multilevel splitting driven by transient duration (channel B), active learning of basin boundaries (channel A), and a uniform share that guarantees the worst-case behavior. Prior sets (channel C) enter as a distribution of starting points and candidates. All arms receive, for each point, only its fingerprint and the label produced by the protocol of Sec.~\ref{sec:protocol}. The strategies do not know which label is the target.

\subsection{Reaction coordinate: transient duration}\label{sec:xi}

For an initial point $x_0$ with a trajectory fingerprint, we define
\begin{equation}
\xi(x_0)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\ln t_{\rm esc}}{\ln N_{\rm tot}}\in[0,1], & \text{escape at time } t_{\rm esc},\\[2mm] 1+D(x_0), & \text{bounded trajectory},\end{cases}
\label{eq:xi}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation*}
D=\frac{\bigl\|(m_{\rm early}-m_{\rm obs},\ s_{\rm early}-s_{\rm obs})\bigr\|}{L}.
\end{equation*}
Here, $N_{\rm tot}$ is the number of integration steps, and $m$ and $s$ are the coordinate-wise means and standard deviations over the early window (the first steps of the transient segment) and over the observation window. A trajectory that quickly settles onto its regime has $D\approx0$. A trajectory that lingers near an edge state on a basin boundary has a large $D$ or a large $t_{\rm esc}$. In rare-event algorithms, the levels are defined by a score (importance) function, and the optimal score is the committor, the probability of reaching the target before the alternatives.\cite{Lestang2018TAMS,Lucente2022Committor} The committor is unknown here; $\xi$ is a cheap proxy computed from a single trajectory that does not use the target label. Following the signal analysis (Appendix~\ref{app:diagnostics}), the coordinate is built on transient rather than asymptotic features. On uniform samples, transient features predict proximity to the target basin with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.88--0.97, whereas asymptotic features ($\lambda$, range) reach only an AUC of 0.5--0.6.

\subsection{Multilevel splitting in the space of initial conditions}\label{sec:sus}

For the static problem ``initial condition $\to$ fingerprint,'' the static counterpart of adaptive multilevel splitting\cite{CerouGuyader2007} is subset simulation.\cite{AuBeck2001} Levels are set adaptively by a quantile of $\xi$, and the sample above a level is replenished by conditional Markov chains. The algorithm is as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Reference sample: $n_0=50$ uniform points of $B$. It is shared with uniform sampling, so that all strategies use the same first 50 random points.
\item The level $\xi_l$ is the value of $\xi$ at the $(n_0p_0)$th largest point, $p_0=0.2$. These $n_0p_0=10$ points are the seeds of the chains.
\item Each chain makes $1/p_0-1=4$ random-walk steps $y=x+\sigma(b-a)\eta$, $\eta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$, with mirror reflection into the parallelepiped $B=[a,b]$. A step is accepted if $\xi(y)\ge\xi_l$. For the uniform measure on $B$ and a symmetric proposal with reflection, this is exactly the Metropolis algorithm for the conditional distribution on $\{\xi\ge\xi_l\}$.
\item The step $\sigma$ (initial value 0.1) is adapted to the acceptance rate $a$, a simple variant of adaptive proposal scaling in subset simulation:\cite{Papaioannou2015} for $a<0.2$, it is halved; for $a<0.4$, it is divided by 1.25; for $a>0.6$, it is multiplied by 1.5.
\item The next level is chosen from all states of the chains of the current level (step 2). On stagnation (a relative increase of the level below 2\% or $\sigma<0.005$) for two levels, and also after 10 levels, the algorithm restarts with a new uniform reference sample.
\end{enumerate}
Restarts protect against the case in which the chains have contracted onto a nontarget region with a long transient. In the benchmark, we compared three coordinates: $\xi$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:xi}, the escape time alone, and the multidimensional ``rarity'' of the fingerprint (the mean distance to the $k=5$ nearest neighbors in the reference sample). Figure~\ref{fig:suschains} shows how the chains of two levels are distributed over the basin map of a Jiang map, and Fig.~\ref{fig:d2mech} shows how the chains in D2 contract to the ball $|w|<\rho$.

\subsection{Active learning of basin boundaries}\label{sec:al}

The active-learning arm stores all evaluated points with their labels; escaping trajectories form a separate class. On these data, a $k$-nearest-neighbor classifier ($k=10$, weights inversely proportional to distance) is trained in normalized coordinates of $B$. From a pool of 2000 candidates, the points with the largest entropy of the predicted class probabilities are selected for evaluation (uncertainty sampling\cite{Settles2012}). Half of the pool consists of Gaussian perturbations of ``boundary'' points of the sample, i.e., points that have a point of another class among their $k$ neighbors, with a scale of half the distance to the nearest neighbor of another class. The other half consists of uniform points or points of a prior set (nullclines). The classifier is retrained when the sample grows by 10\%. In the final configuration (Sec.~\ref{sec:cost}), for $n\ge6$ or while the sample has at most 2000 points, the neighbors are found by brute-force search rather than with a tree, which selects the same queries and is several times faster, and the classifier is trained on the last $2\times10^4$ evaluated points only, so that its cost does not grow with the length of a run. While the data contain a single class, there are no boundaries, and the arm is idle.

\subsection{Portfolio and worst-case guarantee}\label{sec:portfolio}

In each round, the portfolio evaluates a batch of points from the splitting arm (10 points), from the active-learning arm (20 points if the data contain at least two classes), and uniform points. The number of uniform points is $\lceil\varepsilon/(1-\varepsilon)\cdot n_{\rm arm}\rceil$, where $n_{\rm arm}$ is the number of points from the arms and $\varepsilon=0.2$. The order of points within a batch is randomly shuffled, so that no arm gains an advantage in the accounting of the first-hit time. The reference sample is shared by both arms. An arm is called \emph{autonomous} if the choice of its points depends only on the results of its own evaluations. The uniform arm and the splitting arm are autonomous; the active-learning arm uses all data. The uniform share plays the role of the defensive component of mixture importance sampling,\cite{Hesterberg1995} and combining strategies with different failure modes follows the idea of algorithm portfolios, which reduce the risk of heavy-tailed run times.\cite{Huberman1997,GomesSelman2001}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:portfolio}
Let $c$ be the maximum batch size, $w_j$ the guaranteed share of arm $j$ in each round, and $N_{1-\delta}(j)$ the cost of arm $j$ run on its own. Then
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
N_{1-\delta}(\text{portfolio})\le\min\biggl\{&\frac{N^0_{1-\delta}+c}{\varepsilon},\\
&\min_{j\ \text{autonomous}}\frac{N_{1-\delta}(j)+c}{w_j}\biggr\}.
\end{split}
\label{eq:portfolio}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\textit{Proof.} After $N$ evaluations of the portfolio, an autonomous arm $j$ has performed at least $w_jN-c$ evaluations of its own. By autonomy, the sequence of its points is distributed as in a stand-alone run with the same generator. Therefore, $\Pr[\tau>N]\le\Pr[\tau_j>w_jN-c]\le\delta$ whenever $w_jN-c\ge N_{1-\delta}(j)$. For the uniform arm, $N_{1-\delta}(j)=N^0_{1-\delta}$ and $w_j=\varepsilon$. \hfill$\blacksquare$

The consequences are as follows. First, in the worst case, the portfolio is at most $1/\varepsilon=5$ times costlier than uniform sampling. A single adaptive method can be arbitrarily worse: this is how splitting and first-generation novelty search behave, with some runs failing to find the target within the budget (Sec.~\ref{sec:failures}). Second, the scaling exponent of the portfolio is no larger than that of the best autonomous arm: a constant factor $1/w_j$ is lost, but not the order. In our implementation, the share of the splitting arm is $w\approx0.26$ when the boundary arm is active and $w\approx0.77$ without it.

\subsection{Two-stage evaluation}\label{sec:twostage}

Most of the integration budget is spent on trajectories that settle early onto a known regime but are still integrated over the full horizon, which the verification protocol requires for the target (Sec.~\ref{sec:protocol}). We therefore evaluate every initial condition in two stages, for all strategies including uniform sampling. Stage~1 integrates the transient segment up to the end of the early window, $\min(n_{\rm tr},n_{\rm obs})$ steps, followed by a short observation window of $n_{\rm obs}/4$ steps. The trajectory stops after stage~1 if, on this window, it is identified with confidence as a known nontarget regime: an equilibrium (converged, or decaying with an amplitude below $0.02L$), or a known cycle or chaotic attractor with a $z$ score below half the identification tolerance. Escaping trajectories are final anyway. All other trajectories (target candidates and unknown or ambiguous ones) continue from the state reached at stage~1 to the full horizon. Since the main trajectory and the early window are the same, their fingerprints coincide bit for bit with those of a single full integration, and the target is always confirmed by the full protocol. A stopped trajectory keeps its label; only its $\xi$ is computed on the shorter window.

For continued trajectories, stage~1 costs extra steps, because a quarter of the observation window is integrated twice. A rule based on step counts therefore keeps the two-stage mode only while it pays. After the first 200 evaluations, the mode stays on as long as the steps saved over the last ten batches exceed the extra steps; otherwise, only every tenth batch is evaluated in two stages, as a probe. The rule uses step counts rather than time, so runs remain reproducible.

On uniform samples of 1500--4000 points from eight problems, the two-stage labels coincided with those of full integration in all cases, the fingerprints of all continued and escaping trajectories coincided bit for bit, and no target hit was lost. The number of integrator steps fell by a factor of 5.5 on Chua~II, where 94\% of the trajectories settle onto the dominant cycle, by 2.2 on SE11, by 1.9 on LE1, and by 1.4 on two coupled copies of SE11. On Chua~I, the Jafari systems, and D2, nothing is stopped, and the rule switches the mode off. On Chua~II, the distribution of the first-hit time of the portfolio with two-stage evaluation agrees with that of full integration (mean over $R=50$ runs: 74 against 70 integrations).

\subsection{Cost model and channel diagnostics}\label{sec:costmodel}

Subset simulation with selection fraction $p_0$ builds nested sets $S_l=\{\xi\ge\xi_l\}$ with $\mu(S_{l+1}\mid S_l)\approx p_0$; one level costs $n_0(1-p_0)$ evaluations. Denote by $\rho_l=\mu(W^*\mid S_l)$ the fraction of the target in the $l$th set and by $e_l=\rho_{l+1}/\rho_l\in[1,1/p_0]$ the enrichment at level $l$. The target becomes frequent when $\prod_{l<L}e_l\approx1/p$, and the cost is $N\approx n_0+n_0(1-p_0)L+O(1/\rho_L)$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:cost}
If the coordinate is ideal, $e_l=1/p_0$, then $L=\lceil\ln(1/p)/\ln(1/p_0)\rceil$ and $N=O(n_0\ln(1/p))$, i.e., $s=0$. If the enrichment is constant, $e_l=p_0^{-\eta}$ with $0<\eta\le1$, then $L\approx\ln(1/p)/(\eta\ln(1/p_0))$, and the cost is still logarithmic. If the coordinate is informative only down to a depth $L^*$ ($e_l=1$ for $l>L^*$), then $N\approx n_0L^*+\rho_{L^*}^{-1}$. The scaling exponent then returns to $s=1$, with a constant gain $\prod_{l\le L^*}e_l$.
\end{proposition}

Proposition~\ref{prop:cost} follows from counting levels. Its content lies in the classification: an order-of-growth gain requires a coordinate that is informative on all scales, whereas a finite informative depth yields a constant-factor gain. The transient duration diverges at a basin boundary, where the trajectory follows the stable manifold of the edge state. If the target basin is surrounded by such a boundary and the sets $S_l$ are nested all the way down to it, the enrichment persists on all scales (the case of D2). If the boundary of the target does not stand out against other boundaries, or if the duration saturates, only the near zone is informative. Saturation occurs, for example, for the discrete escape times of maps and for escape by slow drift.

\textbf{Diagnostics from the uniform sample.} The enrichment $e_l$ is not observable before the target is detected, but on a benchmark system with a known target, it can be estimated from a uniform sample. What turns out to be decisive is not the fraction of the target in the upper sets $S_q$ (the fraction $q$ of points with the largest $\xi$) but spatial convergence. Let $d(q)$ be the median distance (in units of $L$) from the nontarget points of $S_q$ to the nearest target point of the sample. Splitting gains if $d(q)$ decreases as $q$ decreases: the chains move by a local random walk and need a connected ``path'' of ever longer transients leading to the basin. If $d(q)$ does not decrease, the long trajectories are scattered over the region, and there is no gain even if the fraction of the target in $S_q$ grows (Appendix~\ref{app:diagnostics}).

\textbf{Discretization limit.} The coordinate is built from a trajectory with step $\Delta t$, so escape times shorter than one step are indistinguishable. In D2, the escape time for $|w_0|\gg\rho$ equals $\rho^2/(2\kappa|w_0|^2)$, and the coordinate is informative only for $|w_0|\lesssim\rho/\sqrt{2\kappa\Delta t}$. For $m=1$ and $p=10^{-4}$ ($\rho=6.6\times10^{-4}$), this is 0.5\% of the region, and there is no gain. For $m\ge3$, the informative zone covers a substantial part of the region, and the gain grows with dimension (Appendix~\ref{app:smallp}).
