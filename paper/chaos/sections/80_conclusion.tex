\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusion}

Formulating the search for hidden attractors as rare-event detection makes search methods comparable: the cost $N_{0.9}$ relative to uniform sampling is a single number that any method can report, and uniform sampling itself is a reference whose cost $2.3/p$ is known in advance and is within a constant of the best label-only algorithm. The benchmark, the identification and verification protocol, and the synthetic families with exactly known basin measure are independent of our method and can serve as a common test bed for other localization methods.

The portfolio of splitting driven by transient duration, active learning of basin boundaries, and a uniform share is never much worse than uniform sampling in the number of integrations, gains by factors of 3--7 on the rare hidden attractors of the benchmark that have a transient signal, changes the order of growth of the cost from $p^{-1}$ to $p^{-0.45}$ where the signal is present on all scales, and gains by $10^3$--$10^4$ on coupled copies of systems with hidden attractors. In wall time, the gain carries over where trajectories are bounded, as in the Chua circuits, and shrinks or vanishes where most trajectories escape within a few steps and a uniform point is cheap. The failures of the method are as informative as its gains: they identify the deceptive coordinates, the discrete escape times, the long boundaries, and the time-resolution limit that delineate its domain of applicability. The gains come from the transient duration as a reaction coordinate rather than from the particular way of climbing it: a single stagger-and-step chain on the same coordinate is as strong or stronger where the signal is present and fails where it is absent, so it is a natural arm for the portfolio, whose uniform share keeps the bound.

Beyond the method, the work leaves two tools and one object. The hiddenness measure $h_\varepsilon$ grades hidden attractors by the scale at which the equilibria cease to lead to them; the verification protocol catches both transient chaos mistaken for an attractor and an attractor destroyed by a coarse integration step; and the enumeration of regimes with that protocol revealed a hidden chaotic attractor of two coupled Chua circuits that the single circuit does not have.
