\title[Hidden attractors as a rare-event problem]{Searching for hidden attractors as a rare-event problem: An adaptive strategy portfolio with a worst-case guarantee}

\author{Maksim Elisov}
\email{maksimelisov2003@gmail.com}
\affiliation{Samara National Research University, Moskovskoye Shosse 34, Samara 443086, Russia}

\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
An attractor is hidden if its basin does not intersect small neighborhoods of the equilibria; when the basin is also small, locating the attractor is a rare-event detection problem in the space of initial conditions. We make search methods comparable through the detection probability $P(N)$ after $N$ integrations and the cost $N_{0.9}$ relative to uniform sampling, which needs $2.3/p$ integrations for basin measure $p$, and show that without information from trajectories that miss the target no algorithm beats uniform sampling by more than a factor of about 2.6. Exploiting this information, we propose a portfolio of multilevel splitting driven by transient duration, active learning of basin boundaries, and a uniform share $\theta$; it is guaranteed to cost at most $1/\theta$ times uniform sampling in integrations and inherits the scaling exponent of its splitting arm. On coupled copies of systems with hidden attractors, the portfolio finds rare targets $10^3$--$10^4$ times faster than uniform sampling; on a synthetic family, it reduces the growth of the cost from $p^{-1}$ to $p^{-0.45}$ down to $p=10^{-5}$; on the rare hidden attractors of a four-level benchmark that have a transient signal, it is 3--7 times faster, whereas evolutionary search is no better than uniform sampling. Where trajectories are bounded, the gain persists in wall time: 10--27 times for a rare regime of two coupled Chua circuits. The gain stems from transient duration as a reaction coordinate. A verification protocol that separates attractors from transient chaos reveals a coupling-born hidden chaotic attractor of two coupled Chua circuits.
\end{abstract}

\maketitle

\begin{quotation}
Some attractors of nonlinear systems are hidden: no trajectory started near an equilibrium ever reaches them, so the textbook way of finding attractors misses them. When such an attractor also has a tiny basin, finding it is like searching for a needle in a haystack of initial conditions, and published methods rarely report whether they beat random guessing. This paper recasts the search as a rare-event problem and measures the cost of finding a target with a prescribed confidence. This makes methods comparable and shows that an advantage can come only from what unsuccessful trajectories reveal about the basins. The resulting search strategy is guaranteed never to be much worse than random sampling in the number of trajectories, is several times better on typical problems, and is orders of magnitude better on rare targets in coupled systems. It comes with a benchmark and a verification protocol that catch numerical artifacts and transient chaos mistaken for attractors. Measured in computing time rather than in the number of trajectories, the advantage holds where trajectories stay bounded, as in dissipative physical systems. Applied to two coupled Chua circuits, the protocol reveals a hidden chaotic attractor that a single circuit does not have.
\end{quotation}
