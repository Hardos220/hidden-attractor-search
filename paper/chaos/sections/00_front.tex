\title[Hidden attractors as a rare-event problem]{Searching for hidden attractors as a rare-event problem: An adaptive strategy portfolio with a worst-case guarantee}

\author{Maksim Elisov}
\email{maksimelisov2003@mail.ru}
\affiliation{Samara National Research University, Moskovskoye Shosse 34, Samara 443086, Russia}

\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
An attractor is hidden if its basin does not intersect small neighborhoods of the equilibria, so trajectories launched from unstable equilibria never reach it. When the basin is also small, locating the attractor is a rare-event detection problem in the space of initial conditions. We characterize a search method by its detection probability $P(N)$ after $N$ integrations and its cost $N_{0.9}$ relative to uniform sampling, which needs $2.3/p$ integrations for basin measure $p$; an adaptive method can do better only by using what trajectories that miss the target reveal. We propose a portfolio of multilevel splitting driven by transient duration, active learning of basin boundaries, and a uniform share $\theta$, which is at most $1/\theta$ times costlier than uniform sampling in integrations and inherits the scaling exponent of its splitting arm. On a four-level benchmark with a verification protocol, it reduces the cost of finding rare hidden attractors ($p\sim10^{-3}$--$10^{-2}$) by factors of 3--7 where transients carry a signal; on a synthetic family, the cost scales as $p^{-0.45}$ down to $p=10^{-5}$; on coupled copies of systems with hidden attractors, the gain reaches $10^3$--$10^4$. In wall time, the gain persists for bounded trajectories (10--27 for a rare regime of two coupled Chua circuits) but vanishes where most trajectories escape quickly. A stagger-and-step chain on the same coordinate is as strong where the signal is present: the gain comes from the coordinate, and the portfolio supplies the bound. The identification protocol also reveals a coupling-born hidden chaotic attractor of the coupled circuits.
\end{abstract}

\maketitle

\begin{quotation}
Some attractors of nonlinear systems are hidden: no trajectory started near an equilibrium ever reaches them, so the textbook way of finding attractors misses them. When such an attractor also has a tiny basin, finding it is like searching for a needle in a haystack of initial conditions, and published methods rarely report whether they beat random guessing. This paper recasts the search as a rare-event problem and measures the cost of finding a target with a prescribed confidence. This makes methods comparable and shows that an advantage can come only from what unsuccessful trajectories reveal about the basins. The resulting search strategy is guaranteed never to be much worse than random sampling in the number of trajectories, is several times better on typical problems, and is orders of magnitude better on rare targets in coupled systems. It comes with a benchmark and a verification protocol that catch numerical artifacts and transient chaos mistaken for attractors. Measured in computing time rather than in the number of trajectories, the advantage holds where trajectories stay bounded, as in dissipative physical systems.
\end{quotation}
