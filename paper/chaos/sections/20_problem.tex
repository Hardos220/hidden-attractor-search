\section{Problem formulation}\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Hidden attractors}\label{sec:hidden}

Consider an autonomous system in $\mathbb{R}^n$: a flow $\dot x=F(x)$ or a map $x_{k+1}=F(x_k)$ with a smooth (or piecewise-smooth) right-hand side. Let $A$ be an attractor, $W(A)$ its basin, and $\mathcal{E}$ the set of equilibria (fixed points). The attractor $A$ is \emph{self-excited} if there exists $e\in\mathcal{E}$ such that $W(A)\cap U_\varepsilon(e)\neq\varnothing$ for every $\varepsilon>0$; otherwise, $A$ is \emph{hidden}.\cite{LeonovKuznetsov2013} Three consequences matter for the search. Trajectories launched from neighborhoods of equilibria do not reach a hidden attractor. In systems without equilibria and in systems with a single stable equilibrium, all nontrivial attractors are hidden. A hidden attractor may coexist with self-excited ones, and then its basin is an ``island'' that the unstable manifolds of the equilibria do not reach.

It is convenient to complement the binary definition with a continuous characteristic. Let $\nu_\varepsilon$ be the uniform distribution on the union of the $\varepsilon$-neighborhoods of the equilibria (in an extended version, also on the $\varepsilon$-tubes around their unstable manifolds). We call
\begin{equation}
h_\varepsilon(A)=\Pr_{x\sim\nu_\varepsilon}\bigl[x\in W(A)\bigr]
\label{eq:heps}
\end{equation}
the \emph{hiddenness measure}. For a hidden attractor, $h_\varepsilon(A)=0$ for all small $\varepsilon$, and the function $\varepsilon\mapsto h_\varepsilon$ shows the ``depth'' of hiddenness. Below, it is measured for the benchmark systems (Sec.~\ref{sec:protocol}).

\subsection{Search as rare-event detection}\label{sec:rare}

Let $B\subset\mathbb{R}^n$ be a compact region of initial conditions with normalized Lebesgue measure $\mu$, $\mu(B)=1$. The target attractor $A^*$ has a basin $W^*$ of measure $p=\mu(W^*\cap B)\ll1$. The unit of cost is one integration of standard length $T=T_{\rm tr}+T_{\rm obs}$ (a transient segment and an observation window); for maps, it is one orbit. An algorithm $\mathcal{A}$ chooses initial points $x_1,x_2,\dots\in B$ adaptively: the point $x_k$ may depend on the results of the previous integrations. Let $\tau=\min\{k: x_k\in W^*\}$ be the time of the first hit. The quality of the algorithm is described by the detection probability and by the cost at confidence level $1-\delta$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
&P_{\mathcal A}(N)=\Pr[\tau\le N],\\
&N_{1-\delta}(\mathcal A)=\min\{N: P_{\mathcal A}(N)\ge 1-\delta\}.
\end{split}
\label{eq:pn}
\end{equation}
The probability is taken over the internal randomness of the algorithm; throughout, $\delta=0.1$. The reference point is uniform independent sampling:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
&P_0(N)=1-(1-p)^N,\\
&N^0_{1-\delta}=\left\lceil\frac{\ln\delta}{\ln(1-p)}\right\rceil\approx\frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{p},\qquad N^0_{0.9}\approx\frac{2.3}{p}.
\end{split}
\label{eq:uniform}
\end{equation}
The gain factor $G_{1-\delta}(\mathcal A)=N^0_{1-\delta}/N_{1-\delta}(\mathcal A)$ is equivalent to an ``effective basin measure'' $p_{\rm eff}=pG$. On families with a controlled basin measure, we measure the scaling exponent $s$ in $N_{0.9}\propto p^{-s}$; for uniform sampling, $s=1$. A method gains ``in order'' if $s<1$ with statistical significance and ``by a constant'' if $s\approx1$ but $G>1$.

\subsection{Where a gain can come from}\label{sec:channels}

If an algorithm receives only the attractor label of each point, no adaptivity gives a gain in the worst case: an adversary can place the basin anywhere, and the lower bound on the cost coincides with $1/p$ up to a constant. A gain is possible only through the information carried by the trajectories that did \emph{not} land in $W^*$. We distinguish three channels.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Channel A: basin geometry.} Labels are spatially correlated. If the target basin adjoins already found boundaries between known basins, sampling near these boundaries is enriched with the target.
\item \textbf{Channel B: transients.} Trajectories converging to known attractors or escaping to infinity carry information about proximity to unseen invariant sets: the escape time, delays near saddles, and the mismatch between the early segment of a trajectory and its asymptotic regime. Near a basin boundary, a trajectory follows for a long time the stable manifold of the invariant set on the boundary (the edge state\cite{Skufca2006Edge,Schneider2007Edge,Vollmer2009Edge}), and its transient lengthens. Invariant sets on basin boundaries are classically located by straddle (bisection) methods that exploit exactly this effect.\cite{NusseYorke1989}
\item \textbf{Channel C: prior sets.} Classical heuristics (nullclines, perpetual points, connecting curves) define a distribution of starting points that may be enriched with the target.
\end{itemize}
The proposed method (Sec.~\ref{sec:method}) uses all three channels. The cost model (Sec.~\ref{sec:costmodel}) indicates which channel works for which basin properties and provides an observable diagnostic.

\subsection{What counts as a found attractor}\label{sec:found}

A candidate is accepted if it passes the identification protocol (Sec.~\ref{sec:protocol}). The trajectory must remain bounded and recurrent on the observation window, and its fingerprint must not match the fingerprints of known regimes. Verification includes the lifetime, convergence in the integration step, an independent integrator, and the robustness of the sign of the Lyapunov exponent over windows. For claims of hiddenness, we additionally check that $h_\varepsilon=0$ at several radii. Transient chaos mistaken for an attractor counts as a failure of the method.
