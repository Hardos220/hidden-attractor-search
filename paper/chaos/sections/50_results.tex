\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Measurement protocol}\label{sec:measure}

Each system--strategy pair was run $R$ times with fixed generator seeds: $R=100$ for levels A and B, the synthetic families, and the ablation; $R=50$ for the hyperparameter, horizon, bisection, and enlarged-region tests; and $R=10$--$100$ for the coupled systems. A run stopped at the first hit of the target or when the budget was exhausted. Common random numbers were used: the first 50 points of all strategies coincide with the first 50 points of uniform sampling, so differences arise only after the initial sample. $N_{0.9}$ is the linearly interpolated 0.9 quantile of the first-hit index over the runs (infinity if fewer than 90\% of the runs find the target). Confidence intervals are 95\% bootstrap percentile intervals\cite{EfronTibshirani1994} (2000 resamples of the runs of both the strategy and the uniform reference). The exponent $s$ is the slope of the regression of $\ln N_{0.9}$ on $\ln(1/p)$ over four values of $p$, with its interval from the same bootstrap.

Within each experiment, $N^0_{0.9}$ is remeasured by uniform sampling with the same $R$ and seeds, which is why the uniform costs of Tables~\ref{tab:benchmark}, \ref{tab:gains}, and \ref{tab:scaling} differ by sampling error; the gains of a table are always relative to the uniform runs of the same experiment. The cost tables of Sec.~\ref{sec:cost}, whose runs are few ($R=20$), use instead a fixed reference per system: $N^0_{0.9}$ of Table~\ref{tab:gains} ($R=100$) or the exact $\ln10/p$ where $p$ is known exactly. We compare uniform sampling, Zelinka's DE (Appendix~\ref{app:ablation}), splitting by $\xi$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:xi}, active learning with the 20\% uniform share, the portfolio, and the portfolio with nullclines (channel C), all with full integration. The two-stage configuration of the portfolio (Sec.~\ref{sec:twostage}) is used only in Sec.~\ref{sec:cost}; on runs shorter than $2\times10^4$ evaluations, its cap on the training sample changes nothing. Unless stated otherwise, a gain is the ratio of integration counts.

Uniform sampling for the coupled systems and the enlarged regions was run on a GPU with a kernel that reproduces the CPU trajectories and fingerprints bit for bit. The code was written with the assistance of the AI assistant Claude (Anthropic; used through Claude Code with the models Claude Fable 5.1 and Claude Opus 5.5, 2026) and verified by the bit-exactness tests between the CPU and GPU kernels, by the independent integrators of Sec.~\ref{sec:protocol}, and by the agreement of its labels with Attractors.jl (Sec.~\ref{sec:cost}).

\subsection{Rare targets of levels A and B}\label{sec:rare_ab}

On the three rare targets with a transient signal, Chua~II, SE11, and LE1, the portfolio outperforms uniform sampling by factors of 7.4, 3.1, and 6.2, with the lower bound of the 95\% interval above 1 (Table~\ref{tab:gains}, Fig.~\ref{fig:pn}); with nullcline sampling, it also gains on Chua~I (4.4 times). On LE1, the best single arm is splitting (8.3 times), whereas active learning loses to uniform sampling (0.48): the long ``continuum of equilibria/escape'' boundary attracts the queries, and the target adjoins it only along a small part. The portfolio limits this degradation. On three problems, the portfolio gains little or nothing: the Jafari cycle, the Jafari chaos, and the Huynh map. The cost model explains these failures (Sec.~\ref{sec:costmodel}; they are cataloged in Sec.~\ref{sec:failures}), and the portfolio stays within the bound of Proposition~\ref{prop:portfolio} ($G\gtrsim\theta=0.2$): 0.43, 0.42, and 1.40. Zelinka's DE, which is given the size of the target, is nowhere significantly better than uniform sampling; on the Jafari chaos and cycle, it loses by factors of 4.3 and 3.4. On the Jiang maps ($p\approx0.03$--$0.07$), all strategies are close to uniform sampling: at such $p$, the target is often found within the initial sample, and adaptation has nothing to work with. A single-chain stagger-and-step climb on the same coordinate\cite{SweetNusseYorke2001} is stronger than the splitting arm and than the portfolio where the signal is present (10 on Chua~II) and, without a uniform share, falls below the bound of the portfolio where the coordinate is deceptive or absent (0.16 on the Jafari cycle; Appendix~\ref{app:ablation}): the gain comes from the coordinate, and the role of the portfolio is the bound. The contributions of the arms (ablation), the comparison with bisection toward basin boundaries and with stagger-and-step, and the robustness to hyperparameters and to the integration horizon are given in Appendix~\ref{app:ablation}; no hyperparameter variant brings the gain below 1, and scaling both integration windows by 0.5 or 2 keeps the gains within a factor of 1.7 of the base values.

\subsection{Scaling with basin measure and dimension}\label{sec:scaling_p}

On family D2 ($n=6$), the gain grows as $p$ decreases from $10^{-2}$ to $10^{-5}$ (Table~\ref{tab:scaling}, Fig.~\ref{fig:scalp}). The scaling exponent of the portfolio is $s=0.45$ [0.42, 0.51], and that of splitting by $\xi$ is $0.41$ [0.37, 0.46], against $0.99$ [0.93, 1.04] for active learning and $0.95$ [0.90, 1.03] for uniform sampling (theoretical value 1). On a problem with a signal on all scales, the method thus gains in order of growth: at $p=10^{-5}$, the gain is 93 for splitting and 72 for the portfolio. The exponent is a crossover value over this range rather than the asymptotic $s=0$ of the ideal cost model (Sec.~\ref{sec:costmodel}); a single stagger-and-step chain on the same coordinate reaches $s=0.32$ [0.28, 0.37] (Appendix~\ref{app:ablation}). On family D1, where there is no signal outside the thin rim of the ball, all strategies have $s$ from 0.67 to 0.98; splitting does not help ($G=0.49$--$0.70$), and active learning gives a factor of 1.1--3.5 that grows as $p$ decreases, because escapes inside the ball serve as boundary markers. This is the prediction of Proposition~\ref{prop:cost} for a coordinate with zero informative depth, with the loss below 1 coming from the correlation of the chain states.

Below $p=10^{-5}$, the gain on D2 breaks down: at $p=10^{-6}$, within $2\times10^5$ integrations, the portfolio finds the target in 35\% of runs against 18\% for uniform sampling, and at $10^{-7}$, within $10^6$ integrations, in 16\% against 9.5\%. The breakdown is the discretization limit of Sec.~\ref{sec:costmodel}: the coordinate is informative only in the zone $|w_0|\lesssim\rho/\sqrt{2\kappa\Delta t}$, which occupies 2.3\% of the region at $p=10^{-5}$, 0.23\% at $10^{-6}$, and 0.023\% at $10^{-7}$, so an initial sample of 50 points contains an informative point with probability 0.69, 0.11, and 0.011, and the level threshold of the chains then degenerates (Appendix~\ref{app:smallp}). Reducing the integration step restores the gain: fourfold at $p=10^{-6}$ gives $G=450$ for the portfolio and 530 for splitting, and sixteenfold at $p=10^{-7}$ gives 1200 and 1400, with all runs successful. The limit is set by the time resolution of the reaction coordinate rather than by splitting. The same limit explains the dependence on dimension at fixed $p=10^{-4}$ (Appendix~\ref{app:smallp}): the portfolio gains from 17 times at $n=6$ to 45--61 times at $n=10$--14, but nothing at $n=4$, where the basin in the additional coordinate has width $\rho=6.6\times10^{-4}$ and the coordinate is flat on 99.5\% of the region.

\subsection{Coupled systems: rare hidden targets}\label{sec:coupled}

\textbf{Coupled copies of SE11.} Coupling several copies of a system with a hidden attractor yields a real system with a rare hidden target: the regime in which every copy sits on its hidden attractor. We take $k$ copies of SE11 with mean-field coupling along $y$:
\begin{equation}
\dot{\bm x}_u=\bm F(\bm x_u)+\epsilon\,\bigl(\bar y-y_u\bigr)\,\bm e_y,\qquad u=1,\dots,k,
\label{eq:coupled}
\end{equation}
where $\bar y=k^{-1}\sum_{v}y_v$ and $\epsilon=0.01$, in the region $B^k$. The only equilibrium is the synchronous equilibrium of SE11, which is stable, so the regime ``all copies on the hidden attractor'' is hidden. Its basin measure is $5.5\times10^{-5}$ for $k=2$ ($n=6$) and $3.5\times10^{-7}$ for $k=3$ ($n=9$), close to $p_1^k$ for independent copies ($p_1=0.0079$). Here the portfolio gains by orders of magnitude (Table~\ref{tab:coupled}): 65 \ci{42}{87} at $n=6$ and 1400 \ci{900}{2400} at $n=9$, where it needs about $5\times10^3$ integrations instead of $7.6\times10^6$; all runs found the target. The transient duration is set by the copies that have not yet settled, so successive levels of splitting fix the copies one after another, and the cost grows roughly geometrically in $k$ with a ratio far smaller than $1/p_1$. Without coupling, the gains are the same (1600 at $n=9$), and with four uncoupled copies ($n=12$, $p=p_1^4=3.9\times10^{-9}$), the portfolio finds the target in $N_{0.9}=4.7\times10^4$ integrations, a gain of $1.3\times10^4$ relative to the exact uniform cost $\ln10/p$. Bisection toward basin boundaries does not scale on these systems: within budgets of $8/p$ at $n=6$ and $2\times10^5$ at $n=9$, it finds the target in 66\% and 32\% of runs. The coupling is weak, and the problem is close to a product of independent ones; if the attractors of a single copy are known, product initial conditions reach such a target directly, so the coupled copies test the mechanism of the gain rather than replace that shortcut.

\textbf{Coupled Chua circuits.} Most trajectories of SE11 escape, so its uniform points are cheap (Sec.~\ref{sec:cost}); two mutually coupled Chua~II circuits, with coupling $\epsilon(\bar x-x_u)$ in the first equation and $\epsilon=0.1$, provide a system in which all trajectories are bounded. The nine equilibria (products of the equilibria of the single circuit) are found by Newton's method; the regimes of the coupled system are enumerated by the identification protocol on a uniform sample of $4\times10^4$ points, with the products of the attractors and stable equilibria of the single circuit as initial templates. The synchronized cycle LC takes 98\% of the region. The rare hidden family consists of the regimes in which one circuit is on a hidden chaotic attractor and the other is at a stable equilibrium (and, at weaker coupling, both on hidden attractors); its total basin measure is $2.3\times10^{-5}$. None of the $2\times10^5$ trajectories per radius started in the $10^{-3}L$ and $10^{-2}L$ neighborhoods of the nine equilibria and along their unstable directions reaches it, so $h_\varepsilon<1.5\times10^{-5}$ at the 95\% level, below the measure of the family (zero hits would occur with probability 0.01 if $h_\varepsilon$ were equal to $p$). On this target, the portfolio needs $N_{0.9}=1900$ integrations against $1.05\times10^5$ for uniform sampling ($R=20$, all runs successful), a gain of 55 in integrations; the gain in time is given in Sec.~\ref{sec:cost}. Three uncoupled copies of Chua~II give the extreme case. The regime ``every copy on one of its two hidden attractors'' has $p=(p_a+p_b)^3=1.9\times10^{-8}$ exactly, where $p_a$ and $p_b$ are the measures of the two attractors of the single circuit; this is beyond the reach of uniform sampling ($\ln10/p=1.2\times10^8$ integrations, about two weeks on four threads). The portfolio finds it in 9 of 10 runs within $4\times10^5$ integrations, with a median of $8.6\times10^4$ and $N_{0.9}=3.2\times10^5$ (the largest successful run), a gain of 370 in integrations.

\subsection{A hidden attractor born by the coupling}\label{sec:newattr}

The same enumeration revealed a regime that the single circuit does not have (Fig.~\ref{fig:newattr}): one circuit stays on the large cycle LC, and the other hops chaotically between the regions of the two hidden attractors HA$_a$ and HA$_b$, which in the single circuit at these parameters never merge.\cite{Stankevich2017} Dynamically, it is the chaotic response of the second circuit to the near-periodic drive of the first, with the weak feedback of the coupling; chaos in a periodically driven Chua circuit is classical,\cite{Murali1991DrivenChua} but here the drive is generated by the second copy of the same circuit, and the coupled system is autonomous. The regime passes the verification protocol. Sixteen copies (relative perturbation $10^{-3}$) stay on it for $10^5$ time units and four of them for $10^6$ with RK4; eight copies integrated with DOP853 ($\mathrm{rtol}=10^{-11}$) for $2\times10^4$ time units end on the same template; it has the same template at $\Delta t/2$; and the largest Lyapunov exponent over windows of 100 time units is $0.095\pm0.052$. In 3\%--6\% of the windows, the fingerprint leaves the template during long stays of the second circuit near one of the former attractors, but no copy leaves the regime or decays to another attractor. It is hidden: none of the $2\times10^5$ trajectories per radius started in the $10^{-3}L$ and $10^{-2}L$ neighborhoods of the nine equilibria and along their unstable directions reaches it ($h_\varepsilon<1.5\times10^{-5}$; at $10^{-1}L$, $h_\varepsilon=0.18$, as for Chua~I). Continuation in the coupling shows how it arises: for $\epsilon<0.045$, the second circuit oscillates periodically near one of its stable equilibria (four symmetric periodic regimes); at $\epsilon\approx0.045$, these branches merge into the chaotic hopping; $\lambda$ grows almost linearly to 0.35 at $\epsilon=0.46$, with periodic windows at $\epsilon=0.09$ and $0.47$; and at $\epsilon\approx0.5$, the two branches merge with the synchronized cycle. Coupled Chua circuits with hidden attractors have been studied for synchronization,\cite{Stankevich2017,Kuznetsov2023Chua} and this regime does not appear to have been reported. It is hidden but not rare: its basin fraction is 0.4--1\% per symmetric branch at all $\epsilon$. Table~\ref{tab:newattr} compares how fast the search methods find it: the standard procedure never does, uniform sampling and the portfolio find it within a few hundred integrations, and if the attractors of the single circuit are known, the product initial condition ``LC $\times$ HA$_a$'' lands on it in one integration, because the regime continues that product state. The value of the method here is the enumeration with verification rather than speed.

\subsection{Cost in integrator steps and wall time}\label{sec:cost}

The number of integrations is a fair measure of cost only if all integrations cost about the same. They do not when trajectories escape: an escaping trajectory stops at the step of escape, and the method deliberately concentrates on long-lived trajectories. We therefore measured the cost of the two-stage configuration in integrator steps and wall time (Table~\ref{tab:cost}, Fig.~\ref{fig:cost}). The configuration is the three-arm portfolio with two-stage evaluation (Sec.~\ref{sec:twostage}), brute-force neighbor search, and the capped training sample; for coupled systems, the products of the attractors of the single units other than the target are declared known (``product templates''). The reference is uniform sampling with the same two-stage evaluation at the fixed reference cost $N^0_{0.9}$ of Sec.~\ref{sec:measure}, multiplied by the measured mean cost of a uniform evaluation. Steps are counted with the observation window twice, because the companion trajectory for the Lyapunov exponent is integrated alongside; steps and time up to the first hit are counted in proportion to the position of the hit within its batch, after an untimed warm-up that compiles the kernels. The gains are $G_N$ in integrations, $G_S$ in steps, and $G_T$ in time ($R=20$; three copies of Chua~II: $R=10$), with bootstrap intervals over the runs.

The problems fall into two groups. In the systems where most trajectories escape quickly (Chua~I, SE11, LE1, D2, coupled SE11), the portfolio spends 2--300 times more steps per evaluation than uniform sampling, because it selects exactly the long transients that lead toward the target, whereas most uniform points escape within a few steps. The gain in integrations then shrinks in steps by a factor of 3--300: the portfolio is slower than uniform sampling in time on LE1 and on D2 at $p=10^{-4}$ (upper interval bounds 0.76 and 0.54) and not faster on Chua~I and SE11. It gains in time with statistical significance only where the gain in integrations is very large, on three coupled copies of SE11 ($G_N=3600$, $G_T=29$); on D2 at $p=10^{-5}$ ($G_N=100$), the time gain of 1.5 is not significant (interval [0.27, 3.1]). In the systems with bounded trajectories (Chua~II and its coupled and uncoupled copies), every trajectory runs over the full horizon unless the two-stage evaluation stops it, the steps per evaluation of the portfolio and of uniform sampling are close, and the gain carries over to time: 4.1 on Chua~II, 68 on the coupled circuits, and 130--150 on the uncoupled copies, all with lower interval bounds above 1 except for three uncoupled copies, where one run of ten failed. Two-stage evaluation makes both methods faster, uniform sampling more so: on Chua~II, 94\% of the uniform points and 91\% of the points of the portfolio stop at stage~1; on uniform samples of 1500--4000 points from eight problems, it reduced the number of integrator steps by factors of 5.5 (Chua~II), 2.2 (SE11), 1.9 (LE1), and 1.4 (two coupled copies of SE11), and on Chua~I, the Jafari systems, and D2 the rule switches it off; no target hit was lost, and on Chua~II the first-hit distribution of the portfolio agrees with that of full integration (mean over $R=50$ runs: 74 against 70 integrations). The product templates matter for coupled systems: they let stage~1 stop the trajectories that settle onto product states other than the target and give the active-learning arm informative classes instead of ``unknown.'' On three coupled copies of SE11, they lower $N_{0.9}$ from 3500 to 2100 and raise $G_T$ from 9 to 29; they presuppose that the attractors of the single units are known, and the gain without them, 2200 in integrations and 9 in time, is the one that applies when they are not. The overhead of the arms themselves and the dependence of the time gains on the number of threads are given in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}: the gains of Table~\ref{tab:cost} were measured with four threads per process, and the one-thread values of Table~\ref{tab:time} are smaller by a factor of about 2.5 on the coupled circuits.

\textbf{Comparison with recurrence-based evaluation.} The cost of one evaluation can be reduced further by stopping a trajectory as soon as it recurs in the cells of an already known attractor, as in Attractors.jl.\cite{DatserisWagemakers2022,Datseris2023Framework} For uniform sampling, the index of the first point in the target basin does not depend on how the trajectories are evaluated, so the time of uniform sampling with any evaluation scheme is $N^0_{0.9}$ times the mean cost of one evaluation, and the schemes can be compared on a common set of initial conditions. Table~\ref{tab:time} compares, on one thread, full integration, our two-stage evaluation, and \textsc{AttractorsViaRecurrences} (Attractors.jl 2.0, sparse grid of 100 cells per dimension, sampling interval 0.1, adaptive Tsit5 with tolerance $10^{-9}$ for the Chua systems and fixed-step RK4 with our step for SE11 and LE1, whichever was faster), whose labels on the first 500 points coincide with ours and which recognizes the target states fed to it. On the bounded Chua systems with long transients, recurrence-based stopping is about 3 times cheaper per evaluation than our two-stage evaluation, which still needs the early window and a quarter of the observation window for the fingerprint; on the escaping systems, our evaluation is 2--8 times cheaper, because escape is detected by the radius at once. Against uniform sampling with recurrence-based stopping, the time gain of the portfolio on the coupled Chua circuits falls from 27 to 10, and on Chua~II from 5.7 to 1.8, both still significant; the gain in integrations is untouched. The search strategy and the evaluation back-end are independent choices: the portfolio needs only a regime label and a transient duration for each point, both of which the recurrence mapper provides (Sec.~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\subsection{Failures and limits}\label{sec:failures}

The failures below delineate the domain of applicability of the method.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Deceptive coordinate.} In the Jafari 2026 chaos, almost all trajectories escape to infinity by slow drift, and the longest escapes lie in the region of slow drift rather than near the attractor; splitting finds the target 4.5 times more slowly than uniform sampling ($G=0.22$), the case $e_l<1$ of Proposition~\ref{prop:cost}, and the portfolio stays within the bound (0.42). In an enlarged region, slowly returning trajectories make the coordinate deceptive even for Chua~II: at $k=4$, splitting alone loses by a factor of 7 and the portfolio gives 0.71 (Appendix~\ref{app:enlarged}).
\item \textbf{Escape without a gradient of duration.} For the Jafari cycle, the escape time is determined by slow drift, and for the Huynh map, the escape times are discrete; the target is an ``island'' among escaping trajectories that the transients do not point to. No arm gains on the Jafari cycle, and the portfolio gains only 1.4 times on the Huynh map.
\item \textbf{A long boundary of known basins that the target touches only along a small part (LE1).} The active-learning arm has a median 3.7 times better than uniform sampling but a heavy tail: 14 of 100 runs need more than the uniform cost $N^0_{0.9}=1617$, and two do not find the target within the budget. In the portfolio, this tail is bounded (median 109, maximum 1859 evaluations).
\item \textbf{Time resolution.} The discretization limit at very small $p$ (D2, $p\le10^{-6}$; Sec.~\ref{sec:scaling_p}) and the cheap nontarget trajectories of escaping systems, where a gain of 2--120 in integrations is a draw or a loss in time (Sec.~\ref{sec:cost}), are two faces of the same constraint: the coordinate and the cost are both set by the integrator steps, not by the integrations.
\item \textbf{Single adaptive strategies.} Splitting alone and the earlier strategies of Appendix~\ref{app:ablation} produce heavy tails or fail in a fraction of runs; the uniform share of the portfolio removes exactly these tails.
\end{enumerate}
