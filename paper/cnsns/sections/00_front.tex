\begin{abstract}
Hidden attractors, whose basins avoid small neighborhoods of the equilibria, cannot be located from the equilibria; when their basins are also small, searching for them is a rare-event problem among initial conditions. Measuring cost by the integrations needed to detect the target with prescribed confidence, we propose a search method with a worst-case guarantee and determine when and by how much search beats uniform sampling. The method is a portfolio of multilevel splitting along the duration of transients, active learning of basin boundaries, and a uniform share $\theta$; it provably costs at most about $1/\theta$ times uniform sampling and inherits the cost exponent of each autonomous arm. Its gain is limited by information: if everything an algorithm observes, apart from hitting the target, is constant on a set $Q$ containing the target basin, no algorithm can guarantee a gain larger than about $2.6/\mu(Q)$. For $k$ uncoupled units, this bound and the gain of ideal splitting along the escape time grow geometrically in $k$ with the same base, so adaptive search gains by orders of magnitude on rare hidden regimes of systems of units. On coupled copies of a hidden-attractor system, the portfolio's gain grows from 65 (two copies) to $1.3\times10^4$ (four), with base 14 versus 21 predicted from one copy; on a synthetic family, the cost scales as $p^{-0.45}$ with the basin measure $p$; on bounded systems, transient settling gives gains that persist in wall time. Verified enumeration reveals a coupling-born hidden chaotic attractor of two Chua circuits.
\end{abstract}
